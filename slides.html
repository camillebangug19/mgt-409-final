ReadyHome · MGT 409 final project

When the power goes out, who takes care of everyone?

ReadyHome is a three-minute chat with Sunny that tells families with kids, older adults, people with disabilities and pets how ready they are for a disaster, and the three small steps to take next.

Camille Bangug · AI Foundations for Managers · Fall 2026

Why I built this

This problem started at home.

"When I was younger, my community lost power for a whole week during Hurricane Sandy. Thankfully, we had neighbors to lean on to help take care of all of us."

My household has always held many generations and many needs. Each of them would need something different if we had to leave in a hurry.

Then, as an investor

At the Meng Fund, a student impact investment fund, I scout early-stage climate adaptation and resilience startups.

Real, defensible differentiation is hard to find in this space. But one gap kept showing up:

Almost nothing is built for households with mixed evacuation needs.

The problem

Now picture the same storm, without the neighbors.

One adult. A toddler, a grandparent with a walker and home oxygen, and a dog. No car. The power is out, and so is the cell signal. The family is new to the area, maybe new to the country, with no one nearby to call.

Most Americans (71%) count on friends or family to help after a disaster.1 For isolated families, that safety net doesn't exist, and mixed households are where disasters hit hardest:

71%of Hurricane Katrina deaths were people over 652
13MUS adults aged 50+ say they'd need help to evacuate2
44%of people who stayed behind for Katrina did so because of their pets3

1. FEMA 2024 National Household Survey (copy of FEMA's findings slides, survivingcascadia.com). 2. CDC, Preventing Chronic Disease, 2008. 3. Australian Journal of Emergency Management, 2015.

The real gap is a starting point

Without a baseline, a busy caregiver doesn't know what to do first. My estimate: one hour of guided thinking plus 2–3 hours of gathering changes an evacuation from herculean to manageable.

What's new · demo

A 3-minute chat, a score out of 10, and three small steps.

One real run: a grandparent on home oxygen, no car, in a Florida apartment. 25 chat turns → score 5.0 / 10 → top steps: a transportation plan, backup power for the oxygen, a 7-day medication check. These are exactly the steps the unit tests predict, without calling the model. Every tip links to Ready.gov, CDC, AAP, Red Cross, ACL or HHS.

How the agents work

The AI does the talking. Plain code does the deciding.

Survey Bot (Sunny): runs the chat, one question at a time. It can only save answers through a validated record_answer tool.

Python scoring: turns answers into a 1–10 score and ranks the next steps. It costs no tokens and gives the same answer every time.

Coach: explains why each step matters for this family.

Verifier: lets through only steps and links from the knowledge base.

Model: gpt-6-luna via Portkey · PydanticAI · FastAPI + React

What I did to cut cost

  • Trimmed chat history sent to the model: input tokens 658,691 → 222,214 (−66%), with the same score, the same three steps, and a 25% faster survey.
  • Scoring and ranking run in plain Python, so they cost no tokens.
  • Tap-to-answer buttons, a usage cap on every agent run, and a daily token budget.

Honest notes

Cost fell less than tokens (−12%) because most of the old history was already cached cheaply. Token counts are measured; dollar rates are assumed, since gpt-6-luna pricing isn't published. The projections assume one survey per family per month.

Safety

Built so it can't make things up, or be talked into it.

RiskWhat stops it
Prompt injectionA pre-filter catches it before any model call, so the attack costs nothing
Invented advice or linksThe verifier allows only knowledge-base steps and official URLs
Medical overreachDosing language is blocked; medication steps say "ask your doctor or pharmacist"
Runaway loops or spendUsage limits per run, a 40-turn cap, and a daily token budget (returns HTTP 429)
PrivacyZIP + state only. No names, no street address, no accounts

A real user

Tested with the person at home who thinks about this most.

My fiancé doesn't work in disaster management; he works in AI and data science. But he cares a lot about natural disaster preparedness and buys many of our family's emergency supplies, so he was the ideal first tester. Our household: two adults and a pet in Connecticut.

2.5 → 3.5score after one step (7-day meds check)
6 minstart to results, 26 questions

"Wow, I'm realising we have none of this stuff… we should get it together!"

Even the person who stocks our supplies found big gaps. The gap was never motivation; it was knowing where to start.

His feedback was valuable, and specific

  • "The name isn't anywhere on the page." Fixed during the session: the ReadyHome wordmark is now on every screen.
  • "Chain of questions is redundant." Plan, meeting place and out-of-area contact felt like one question. Next: group them.
  • "Should the scores mean something?" A 2.5 needs a reference point.
  • "It is a little slow" and "Is this financial advice?" Both fair: ~5 seconds per question, and the disclaimer only covers medical advice.
  • "Curious if it works for people who actually have equipment evac needs." The sharpest one, and still untested with a real household.
What's next

Next, with another month.

  • Test with households that have the needs it was built for: power-dependent equipment, wheelchairs, an infant. My tester named this himself, and it comes first.
  • Shorter survey: group related questions to get below 26.
  • A reference point for the score ("households like yours usually start around…").
  • More languages, so every family can take the survey in the language they speak at home.
  • ZIP → county for local hazards and county .gov links, in all 50 states.
  • Reminders for formula expiry and registry renewal, and a shareable plan for caregivers and neighbors.

Why it matters

At about $0.15 a family, a county emergency office or a community nonprofit could offer this to 10,000 households for roughly $1,500 a month.

A shareable plan rebuilds the neighbor network that got my family through Sandy, for the families who don't have one.

Being prepared isn't about being perfect. It's about taking the next small step.
